What Is the Milky Way?

The Milky Way is a large barred spiral galaxy containing stars, planets, gas, dust, stellar remnants, and dark matter. From Earth, we see the galaxy as a broad band of diffuse light across the night sky because the Solar System is located inside its disk.

Because we are inside the Milky Way, astronomers cannot photograph the entire galaxy from an outside viewpoint. Instead, they combine observations of stars, gas, dust, infrared light, radio waves and stellar motion to reconstruct its large-scale structure.

How Do Astronomers Study the Milky Way?

Studying the Milky Way is different from studying distant galaxies because Earth is located inside it. Dust can block visible light, while the galaxy's enormous scale makes its complete structure difficult to observe directly.

Astronomers therefore combine different types of observations. Infrared observations can reveal stars hidden behind dust, radio observations trace cold gas and hydrogen, and measurements of stellar positions and velocities help map the galaxy's structure and rotation.

Structure of the Milky Way

The Milky Way contains several major components: a flattened stellar disk, a central bulge and bar, spiral structure, a surrounding stellar halo, and an extended dark-matter halo.

Galactic Disk

A thin, rotating region containing much of the galaxy's stars, gas and dust.

Central Bulge & Bar

A dense central region surrounding the Milky Way's innermost area.

Spiral Arms

Regions of enhanced stellar and gas density extending through the galactic disk.

Galactic Halo

A much larger, roughly spherical region surrounding the disk and containing old stars and globular clusters.

Sagittarius A*: The Milky Way's Central Black Hole

At the center of the Milky Way lies Sagittarius A*, a supermassive black hole. It has a mass of roughly four million Suns and is located about 26,000 light-years from Earth.

Astronomers established the presence of this compact massive object by observing the rapid orbits of stars around the galactic center. These stellar motions provide strong evidence for an extremely massive object concentrated in a very small region.

Where Is the Solar System?

The Solar System lies within the Milky Way's disk, roughly 26,000 light-years from the galactic center. We are located in the Orion–Cygnus region, between major spiral-arm structures.

Our position gives astronomers an unusual perspective: we can study individual stars in great detail while simultaneously trying to reconstruct the overall structure of our galaxy.

Galaxy Classification

The Milky Way is classified as a barred spiral galaxy. In this class, a central bar of stars crosses the galactic center and connects to the surrounding spiral structure. NASA describes the Milky Way and Andromeda as barred spirals, although the detailed shape of our own galaxy is still being refined because we observe it from inside.

Why the classification matters: a galaxy's shape reflects its stars, gas, dust, gravity, and evolutionary history. The bar and spiral structure are dynamic features rather than perfectly rigid structures.

Central Bulge & Bar

Galactic Bulge

The bulge is a dense central concentration of mostly older stars extending around the inner galaxy. It is much thicker than the thin stellar disk and is difficult to map directly because dust obscures the galactic center.

Central Bar

The Milky Way has a long stellar bar through its central region. Spiral structure connects to this bar, while gas and stars move through the central gravitational environment.

Spiral Arms

The Milky Way's spiral structure contains major and smaller arm-like features. NASA's current educational material highlights the Scutum–Centaurus and Perseus arms as major structures, with Sagittarius and Norma treated as less prominent arms. Our Solar System lies in the smaller Orion Spur, between the Sagittarius and Perseus regions.

Scutum–Centaurus

A major spiral structure extending from the central region.

Perseus

A major outer spiral structure traced by stars, gas and star-forming regions.

Sagittarius

A prominent inner structure associated with gas and star formation.

Orion Spur

The local partial arm where the Sun and Solar System are located.

Galactic Disk

The disk is a flattened, rotating component containing much of the Milky Way's visible stars, gas and dust. Most active star formation occurs in gas-rich regions within the disk. The Sun orbits the galactic center at roughly 828,000 km/h and takes about 230 million years to complete one revolution.

Stellar Halo

Surrounding the disk is a much larger, roughly spherical stellar halo populated by old stars and globular clusters. The halo is sparse compared with the disk, but it preserves evidence of the Milky Way's formation and past mergers with smaller galaxies. Modern surveys such as Gaia are helping astronomers map this region through stellar motions.

Dark Matter Halo

The Milky Way is embedded in an enormous dark-matter halo. Dark matter does not emit or reflect light, so astronomers infer it from gravitational effects on stars, gas, satellite galaxies and larger-scale structure. NASA visualizations describe the dark-matter halo as extending far beyond the visible stellar disk.

Scientific uncertainty: the exact distribution and total mass of the Milky Way's dark-matter halo are still active research questions. The halo is inferred from gravity, not photographed directly.

Star Formation

Stars form when cold, dense regions of interstellar gas collapse under gravity. In the Milky Way, giant molecular clouds provide reservoirs of material from which new stars and planetary systems can develop. Young stars illuminate nearby gas and dust, making active star-forming regions especially useful laboratories for understanding stellar birth.

Infrared observations are particularly important because dust can hide young stars at visible wavelengths. NASA notes that Webb's infrared instruments are improving our understanding of the stellar life cycle, from birth through later stages.

Future of the Milky Way

The Milky Way will continue to evolve through star formation, stellar deaths, satellite-galaxy interactions and mergers. Gaia and other surveys are revealing streams and disturbances in the halo that record past encounters with smaller galaxies. Future observatories will refine our picture of the galaxy's structure and motion.

Milky Way + Andromeda

Andromeda (M31) is the nearest large galactic neighbor of the Milky Way at roughly 2.5 million light-years away. The two galaxies are gravitationally interacting, but the probability and timing of a future merger remain sensitive to measurements of their positions, proper motions and the influence of other Local Group galaxies.

Current scientific picture: a 2025 peer-reviewed study using Hubble and Gaia measurements found the long-term outcome remains uncertain, with close to a 50% chance of no Milky Way–Andromeda merger within 10 billion years. A 2026 preprint using updated Gaia-based measurements reports a substantially higher merger probability in its preferred model. These results are not yet a settled consensus, so SpaceVerse presents the outcome as an active research question rather than a certainty.

Milky Way FAQ

How old is the Milky Way?

The Milky Way formed very early in cosmic history and is approximately 13.6 billion years old.

How large is the Milky Way?

The visible stellar disk is commonly estimated to span roughly 100,000–120,000 light-years, although the galaxy's extended halo is much larger.

What is at the center of the Milky Way?

Sagittarius A*, a supermassive black hole with a mass of about four million Suns, lies at the center.

Where is Earth in the Milky Way?

Earth is in the Solar System, located roughly 26,000 light-years from the galactic center within the galaxy's disk.

Can we photograph the Milky Way from outside?

No. We are inside the galaxy, so astronomers reconstruct its overall structure using observations from many wavelengths and measurements of stars and gas.

Will the Milky Way change in the future?

Yes. Galaxies evolve continuously through star formation, stellar evolution, interactions and mergers.
