Kepler-90 System

A compact eight-planet system around the Sun-like star Kepler-90 in Draco. Its discovery is famous because Kepler-90 became the first exoplanet system known to match our Solar System's eight-planet count. This panel separates measured properties from interpretation and unknowns.

The Kepler-90 star

Sun-like host

Kepler-90 is a G-type main-sequence star. NASA describes it as Sun-like and slightly larger/more massive than the Sun.

Planetary environment

All eight known planets orbit much closer to Kepler-90 than the equivalent planets in our Solar System orbit the Sun, making the architecture extremely compact.

Why it matters

The system provides a natural laboratory for studying how many planets can form, migrate and remain dynamically organized around one star.

Compact orbital architecture

Rocky inner worlds

The inner planets are relatively small and highly irradiated. NASA compares the overall layout with our Solar System: small planets inside and larger worlds farther out.

Large outer worlds

The outer members are larger and more gaseous in character. Kepler-90 h is the outermost known planet and orbits at roughly the Earth-Sun distance.

Everything is compressed

NASA notes that all eight planets could fit inside Earth's orbit around the Sun. The resemblance to our Solar System is therefore mainly in planet count and broad ordering, not scale.

Discovery history

Kepler finds the system

Transit signals from multiple planets were identified in NASA Kepler observations. The planets were validated over several years as the system's architecture became clearer.

2017 breakthrough

Researchers Christopher Shallue and Andrew Vanderburg used a neural-network approach to re-examine archived Kepler data and found the weak transit signal of Kepler-90 i.

Why AI mattered

The Kepler archive contains enormous amounts of data. Machine learning demonstrated that previously missed planetary signals can be recovered from those archives, complementing traditional analysis.

Kepler-90 i and machine learning

Kepler-90 i is the system's famous eighth planet. NASA's catalog currently lists it as a super-Earth with a radius of about 1.32 Earths, a mass of about 2.3 Earths, a 14.4-day orbital period and an orbital radius of 0.120138 AU. Its discovery was announced in 2017 and used the transit method. It is a very hot world and is not considered a good candidate for life.

Habitability & climate

Not an Earth twin

NASA says none of the Kepler-90 planets are considered good candidates for life. The compact system places the rocky inner worlds under intense stellar irradiation.

Habitable-zone caution

A planet's location alone cannot establish habitability. Atmosphere, pressure, chemistry, surface conditions and long-term climate stability are all required to assess whether liquid water could persist.

Outer worlds

The cooler outer planets are more distant, but their larger/gaseous nature makes them poor Earth-like targets. A hypothetical rocky moon would be a separate question and is not an observed feature.

Formation, migration & dynamics

Kepler-90's compact architecture is useful for studying planetary formation and migration. The fact that small planets occupy the inner region while larger planets lie farther out resembles the broad organization of our Solar System, but every orbit is much tighter. The exact formation history, migration pathways and long-term dynamical evolution are active areas of research rather than directly observed historical events.

Kepler-90 vs. our Solar System

Similar

Both systems have eight known planets, with smaller inner worlds and larger outer worlds.

Different

Kepler-90 is vastly more compact: NASA notes that all eight planets would fit inside Earth's orbit around the Sun.

Habitability

Our Solar System contains Earth; NASA currently considers the known Kepler-90 planets too hot or otherwise unsuitable as strong life-search targets.

What we still do not know

Atmospheres

Detailed atmospheric compositions and cloud structures are not known for the planets from direct atmospheric spectroscopy.

Moons & rings

No specific moons or rings have been confirmed in this system. Any such objects shown in an artistic visualization are hypothetical.

True interiors

Mass-radius relationships constrain the planets, but their detailed cores, mantles, envelopes and compositions remain model-dependent.
