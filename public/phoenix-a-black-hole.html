Phoenix A — Supermassive Black Hole at the Heart of the Phoenix Cluster

Phoenix A is the extreme central black hole in the galaxy at the heart of the Phoenix Cluster, a massive galaxy cluster about 5.8 billion light-years from Earth. The system is scientifically important because its powerful black-hole activity, surrounding hot gas, and unusually rapid star formation provide a rare view of how black-hole feedback can influence an entire cluster environment.

What is Phoenix A?

Phoenix A is the central supermassive black hole associated with the brightest central galaxy of the Phoenix Cluster. It is not a freestanding object floating in empty space: it is embedded in a dense, hot, multi-galaxy environment containing enormous reservoirs of intracluster gas. Material falling toward the black hole can power an active galactic nucleus, producing jets and other energetic feedback that interact with the surrounding gas.

Structure / Composition

A black hole itself is described by its mass and spacetime geometry rather than a solid surface or chemical composition. Around Phoenix A, the observable structure includes a hot intracluster medium, an accretion-powered central engine, energetic jets, radio-emitting structures and cooler filaments of gas. Chandra observations revealed cavities in the hot gas associated with the black hole's outbursts, while radio observations trace the jets that inflate those cavities.

Formation

Phoenix A's exact growth history is not directly observed. Like other supermassive black holes, it likely grew through a combination of accretion and mergers over cosmic time. Its exceptionally massive cluster environment provides a large reservoir of gas and frequent gravitational interactions, but the relative contributions of different growth channels remain an active research problem.

How scientists discovered and studied it

The Phoenix Cluster was first identified in Sunyaev–Zeldovich observations and was subsequently studied at X-ray, optical, ultraviolet, millimeter and radio wavelengths. NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory revealed the extreme hot-gas and cooling environment, while Hubble observations showed filaments of cooler gas and star-forming structures. Radio observations with the Very Large Array and ALMA traced energetic jets and cold gas, and newer Webb observations are helping astronomers build a more complete picture of the cluster's star-formation cycle.

Why it matters

Phoenix is a remarkable test case for the relationship between black holes and star formation. Normally, powerful black-hole outbursts can heat cluster gas and prevent it from cooling enough to form stars. In Phoenix, observations show an unusual regime in which black-hole activity is not fully suppressing cooling and, in some regions, appears connected to enhanced cooling and star formation. Chandra reported star formation in the central cluster at roughly hundreds of solar masses per year, making the system one of the clearest laboratories for studying this feedback cycle.

Current research

Current work combines data from Chandra, Hubble, radio observatories, ALMA and the James Webb Space Telescope. Webb observations published in 2025 helped clarify how the Phoenix Cluster is assembling and how cool gas and star formation are connected to its broader environment. Researchers are continuing to study how the black hole's jets transfer energy and momentum to hot gas, how gas cools into molecular reservoirs, and how the balance between heating and cooling changes over time.

What we know

  • The Phoenix Cluster is about 5.8 billion light-years from Earth.
  • Its central galaxy hosts a very massive supermassive black hole.
  • The black hole powers energetic outflows and jets visible through radio and X-ray observations.
  • Chandra has detected cavities in the hot intracluster gas associated with those outbursts.
  • The Phoenix Cluster has an unusually high rate of central star formation for a massive galaxy cluster.
  • Multiwavelength observations show a complex interaction between hot gas, cooler filaments, jets and newly forming stars.
  • NASA's Webb observations are adding new detail to the cluster's gas and star-formation history.

What remains uncertain

Phoenix A is much better described as an extreme central active black hole than as a precisely weighed “100-billion-solar-mass” object. Published estimates can differ substantially depending on the assumptions and measurements used. NASA's 2025 Phoenix Cluster summary describes a black hole of roughly 10 billion solar masses, while older studies and popular summaries have quoted substantially larger values. SpaceVerse therefore presents the mass as an estimate rather than a settled exact number. The detailed growth history, geometry of the accretion flow and long-term balance between black-hole heating and cluster cooling also remain active areas of research.
