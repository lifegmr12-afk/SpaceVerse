Sagittarius A* — the supermassive black hole at the heart of the Milky Way

Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*) is the supermassive black hole at the center of our galaxy. It lies roughly 26,000–27,000 light-years from Earth in the direction of Sagittarius. Although it is currently a relatively quiet galactic nucleus, the motion of nearby stars and gas gives astronomers an exceptionally detailed view of gravity in the strongest environment available in the Milky Way.

What is Sagittarius A*?

Sagittarius A* is a compact object whose gravitational mass is millions of times greater than the Sun's. It is not a normal star and has no visible solid surface. The familiar name refers to the compact radio source and the black hole at the center of the Milky Way. Unlike a luminous quasar, Sgr A* currently accretes relatively little matter, so its immediate environment is far dimmer than those of the brightest active galactic nuclei.

Structure / Composition

The black hole itself is surrounded by a hot, magnetized accretion flow made of extremely thin plasma. Individual stars orbit at great speed around the galactic center, while transient gas structures and flares can appear near the black hole. The Event Horizon Telescope resolves emission from a region only a few times the black hole's event-horizon scale, allowing scientists to study its shadow and strongly lensed light.

Formation

The exact origin of the Milky Way's central black hole remains an active research problem. It likely grew through a combination of early black-hole seeds, gas accretion and mergers as the Milky Way assembled. The present-day mass reflects billions of years of growth, while the details of its earliest seed and its full merger history cannot be observed directly.

How scientists discovered it

Sgr A* was first identified as a compact radio source in the center of the Milky Way. The strongest evidence for a massive black hole came from long-term observations of individual stars, especially the fast-moving S stars. Their tightly bound orbits reveal that about four million solar masses are concentrated in a tiny region. Infrared and radio interferometry later allowed researchers to observe the immediate environment and, in 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope published the first image of Sgr A*'s ring-like emission.

Why it matters

Sagittarius A* is one of the best laboratories for testing general relativity and black-hole astrophysics. Because it is in our own galaxy, scientists can follow individual stars over many years rather than relying only on unresolved light from distant galaxies. Its proximity also makes it possible to study magnetic fields, plasma turbulence, accretion physics and extreme gravitational lensing in detail.

Current research

Current observations combine the Event Horizon Telescope, Chandra X-ray Observatory, James Webb Space Telescope, Very Large Telescope and other facilities. Researchers are studying the black hole's changing accretion flow, magnetic-field structure, infrared and X-ray flares, stellar orbits, and the behavior of hot plasma near the event horizon. New observing campaigns are also testing whether the observed ring structure changes over time and how closely models match the data.

What we know

  • Sgr A* is a supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way.
  • Its mass is about four million times the mass of the Sun, based largely on stellar-orbit measurements.
  • It is roughly 26,000–27,000 light-years from Earth.
  • The surrounding accretion flow is relatively faint compared with active quasars.
  • Stars orbiting the galactic center provide direct dynamical evidence for the compact mass.
  • The Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of Sgr A* in 2022.
  • The observed emission ring is produced by hot plasma and strongly lensed light around the black hole.

What remains uncertain

The exact black-hole spin, detailed magnetic-field topology, three-dimensional structure of the accretion flow, and the black hole's long-term feeding history are not fully determined. Even the apparent diameter and shape of the EHT ring depend on the plasma model, scattering corrections, calibration and observing conditions.
